# Easy-to-Roleplay

Make every NPC a performance, not a description. This skill generates characters through two lenses: a **Roleplay Prompt** that is the scene premise — specific, immediately actable, comic or dramatic engine already installed — and an **Anchor** that tells you how to sound doing it.

---

## The Two-Part System

### Part 1: The Roleplay Prompt

The Roleplay Prompt is a single sentence. Not a vibe. Not a summary. The **specific, ironic, or dramatically loaded thing that is true about this character right now** — the thing that makes them playable the moment it's read.

The prompt is modeled on the show *Make Some Noise* (Dropout TV), where every prompt is a one-liner that contains the whole scene inside it. The goal is the same: read it once, know exactly what to do, start performing.

**What it looks like — situational:**
- *A sommelier who's never tasted wine*
- *A werewolf who only turns into a wolf when their account is overdrawn*
- *Turns out the hostage negotiator knows the hostage taker from way back*
- *A palm reader trying to sugarcoat very bad news*
- *The more tools the surgeon asks for, the clearer it is he's winging it*
- *A villain and their real estate agent tour volcano lairs*
- *A siren who refuses to admit she's past her sailor-luring days*
- *Both of these people are definitely wearing a wire*

**What it looks like — pop culture reference baked in:**
- *Jennifer Coolidge, air traffic controller*
- *David Attenborough, but he fucking hates toads*
- *Zooey Deschanel as the world's quirkiest assassin*
- *Christoph Waltz threateningly offers to shovel your driveway*
- *Seth Rogen hosting Cosmos*
- *Nick Offerman directing all the animals onto Noah's Ark*

Pop culture references are not just for the Anchor — they can BE the Roleplay Prompt when the reference instantly communicates both the character's voice AND the ironic situation. "Jennifer Coolidge, air traffic controller" tells you everything: the drawl, the oblivious authority, the chaos. You don't need more. Use a pop culture reference in the Prompt whenever it does more work than a description would.

**What the prompt needs to contain:**
- **The character** — their role, identity, expertise, or a pop culture reference that nails the energy
- **The specific thing that makes them *this* version** — the irony, contradiction, secret, situation, or hidden pressure right now

The second part is everything. "A court wizard" is nothing. "A court wizard who is incredibly good at magic and completely wrong about how magic works" is a whole performance. The specificity IS the joke, the tension, or the human moment. A pop culture reference earns its place when it delivers that specificity in fewer words.

**Prompt structures that work (from MSN):**
1. **[Character] who [ironic condition]** — "A sommelier who's never tasted wine"
2. **[Pop culture figure] + [impossible role]** — "Jennifer Coolidge, air traffic controller"
3. **[Pop culture figure], but [specific twist]** — "David Attenborough, but he fucking hates toads"
4. **[Two-person scene] with [hidden tension]** — "Turns out the hostage negotiator knows the hostage taker from way back"
5. **[Situation] escalating in a specific direction** — "The more tools the surgeon asks for, the clearer it is he's winging it"
6. **[Familiar figure] in [specific absurd moment]** — "Three soldiers bored inside the Trojan Horse"

**What makes a prompt good:**
- Read it cold and know immediately what to do
- The bit is in the premise, not in the execution
- Specific over general — not "a suspicious merchant" but "a merchant who is clearly selling something illegal but is also, visibly, the most honest person in this entire market"
- The word "clearly" does a lot of work — it tells you the character is trying to hide something and failing
- Built-in contradiction: two things that shouldn't coexist but do

**What to avoid:**
- Vague stacked adjectives ("a brooding, mysterious, dangerous rogue")
- Prompts where the funny is just the noun ("a skeleton who works at a bank" — push further: "a skeleton loan officer who is increasingly unclear on whether the currency he issued in 1347 is still valid")
- Describing behavior instead of the situation ("a merchant who acts shady" → find the specific shady thing)
- Prompts that require explanation to be funny

### Part 2: The Anchor

The Anchor is a pop culture mashup that locks in **voice, cadence, and energy**. It answers: *how does this character sound and move?*

**Formula:** `[unexpected thing/archetype/state] + [pop culture character or persona]`

**Examples:**
- *Burned-out high school vice principal Voldemort* — deeply tired, still menacing, way too invested in the rules
- *Rat grandma Scarface* — foul-mouthed, territorial, weirdly loving; calls everyone "baby" right before threatening them
- *Southern golden retriever lawyer* — aggressively friendly, relentless, genuinely cannot tell you don't like him
- *Pirate dad at a BBQ competition* — gregarious, competitive, immediately emotional about smoked brisket
- *Yoga instructor who is also a hitman* — calm, centered, pivots to violence without changing her tone
- *Owl Obi-Wan Kenobi* — slow, cryptic, warm; speaks in half-finished wisdom; lets silence do the heavy lifting

The Anchor is the **operating system for delivery**. The Prompt tells you what to play; the Anchor tells you how it sounds.

**When to use the Anchor:** Always include it, but it does the most work when the Roleplay Prompt doesn't already carry a pop culture reference. If the Prompt is "Jennifer Coolidge, air traffic controller," the Anchor is mostly redundant — Jennifer IS the anchor. If the Prompt is "A harbormaster who has been waiting years to enforce this one regulation," the Anchor is essential — add something like "mid-season Tony Soprano running an HOA meeting" to lock in the delivery.

The Anchor doesn't need to match the character's world. A medieval innkeeper can have a Frasier Crane anchor. A sea monster can have a David Attenborough anchor. What matters is that the DM can hear the voice immediately.

**Good Anchor traits:**
- Immediately playable — the DM can hear the voice in their head instantly
- Surprising but coherent — the two halves click
- Creates built-in contradictions to play with
- Specific enough to suggest actual line delivery

**Avoid:**
- Generic archetypes ("wise old wizard" — that's not an Anchor, it's the character)
- So obscure it doesn't land
- Anchors that reduce the character to a one-note gag

## Generating the Concept

**Two modes:**

- **DM-provided:** User gives you a premise or Anchor direction. Execute on it — build the Prompt to match, don't second-guess it.
- **Generated:** No concept given. Generate **2–3 Roleplay Prompts** (one sentence each, no elaboration), let the DM pick, then execute the full output on the chosen one.

When generating prompts from scratch, aim for built-in contradiction or irony over pure absurdity. The best table characters are the ones that are funny *and* a little sad, or tense *and* a little ridiculous.

## Output Format

### [Character Name]

**Roleplay Prompt:** [The MSN-style one-liner — situationally specific, immediately actable]

**Anchor:** [The mashup — e.g., "Burned-out high school vice principal Voldemort"]
> *[One sentence on why this Anchor nails the delivery for this character]*

**Voice & Delivery**
- Speech patterns, vocabulary, verbal tics
- 2–3 actual lines the DM can say at the table
- Physical mannerisms (hands, eye contact, posture)
- Emotional default state + what cracks it

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| **Primary Goal** | What they always want, in their voice |
| **Consistent Method** | How they always pursue it — their MO |
| **Active Problem** | What's currently going wrong for them |
| **Performance Hooks** | 2–3 specific DM moves: when to lean in, when to break, when to surprise |
| **Link of Relevance** | Why the party should care — their hook into the story |
| **Off-Screen Action** | What they're doing when the party isn't watching |

## Layering with Other Skills

When co-firing with **prep-npc**, **prep-monster**, or **blm-prep-style**:
- The Roleplay Prompt is the first thing in the output — above everything
- The Anchor + Voice & Delivery replaces or augments Performance Hooks
- BLM toy fields (Goal, Method, Problem, Links) are rewritten in the character's voice — not clinical summaries, but felt

When used **standalone** on an existing character:
- Read what's already there
- Find the specific, ironic, or dramatically loaded thing that is actually true about them
- Generate the Prompt around that — don't describe the character, name their specific situation
- Generate the Anchor separately for delivery
- Rewrite the full output around both

## Tone Principles

- **The prompt should do the work.** If the DM needs to do creative lifting after reading the Roleplay Prompt, the prompt failed.
- **Specific over general.** "Speaks like he's reading his own obituary and enjoying it" > "speaks slowly and dramatically."
- **The bit is in the premise.** Everything in Voice & Delivery should feel like a natural consequence of the Roleplay Prompt, not a surprise.
- **Contradiction is the hook.** Funny and sad. Terrifying and pathetic. Helpful and suspicious. Two things at once.
- **Give the DM lines, not descriptions.** Don't say "she's sarcastic" — write a sarcastic thing she'd actually say.
- **Performance notes are physical.** What does this character do with their hands? Do they make eye contact? Do they laugh at their own jokes?

## Sandbox Campaign Constraint

The Shattered Sea is a sandbox. NPCs have their own agendas that exist independently of the players — they are not waiting for the party to arrive, and their goals don't center on party decisions.

When writing NPC content for this campaign:

- **Off-Screen Action** must reflect what the NPC would be doing *regardless of player choices*. If it only makes sense if the party did something specific, it's not sandbox — it's railroading.
- **Link of Relevance** should describe how the NPC's agenda *intersects with* the party's path, not how the party *needs* to engage the NPC.
- **Never prescribe player reactions.** "The party will find her charming" or "this NPC will be the party's guide" — rewrite these. Write what the NPC offers; leave what the party does with it completely open.
- **Performance Hooks** should be about what the NPC does, not what the players are supposed to feel or do in response.

The NPC is a person with a life. The party is a variable that entered that life. Write the person.

Also load `creative-writing-craft` and apply its **Sandbox Campaign Constraints** section when writing any narrative content for this character.
