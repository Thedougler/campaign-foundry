## Tone

Dark heroic fantasy. Heroism costs. Victories are real and they leave a mark.

The Shattered Sea's own register: swashbuckling polish complicated by pettiness. Capable people make real danger, and vanity, resentment and immature needs wreck their best work. High competence sits inside low emotional maturity.

Build each major character from one public image, one real competence, one humiliating need and one petty fixation. Build each major scene from one serious external threat and one petty internal conflict: a boarding action that pauses over bad gunpowder, a treaty that fails on seating and décor. Banter is social combat, with interruption, precision insults, status testing and old slights never forgotten.

Keep glamour, danger, decadence and sharp dialogue, with consequences that stick. Comedy comes from the characters' flaws. The sea and its monsters can still kill, and the people are the disasters. Never give two NPCs the same sarcastic voice, and never let a joke erase a consequence. Skip broad clown comedy, parody and non-stop one-liners.

NPC dialogue defaults to a register that tests status and guards real needs. Expect competent professionals undone by interpersonal baggage.

This guidance governs narrative style. Lines and Veils sets hard content boundaries. Tone never overrides them, accessibility, safety or an owner page's established truth.

## Themes

The Campaign is about the different kinds of authority and who should wield it. Rebellion against authority is its central drive, and over the Campaign the Party takes on increasingly greater forms of authority.

Authority climbs rungs. Authorities come first, then lords, then kings, then false gods or demigods, then gods. The lowest rung, the authorities, are people who carry out a system's will without owning any of it. They enforce it because they believe in it or like it, rather than for the wage. The examples repeat at every system, from the rating stamp of the harbour inspector to the basin book of the [[Waveservants|Waveservant]] collector, the terms of the Concordat clerk and the tallies of the blue-caste handler on a grung beach. The better deal tells them apart: an authority refuses it, and an ordinary person leaves. Lords such as [[Barnaby Rook]] and [[Aleksander Malone]] command one vessel, fort or small group of men. Kings wield entire nations or companies. The Gold caste of the Grung are the false gods. The gods and the sea stand above every rung, and [[Umberlee]] is both at once.

Each power stands for a kind of authority. The [[Dravosi Crown]] works at the local level and represents might making right. The [[Tessarine Concordat]] stands higher and rules through systems, a passive authority. The Grung enslave people and take their agency, while the caste's own magic figuratively enslaves the Grung to the [[Gold Caste]]. [[Umberlee]] is tempestuous and petty, and her title came from her wrath and her greed. She deserves no worship.

Authority claimed from above stands against authority given from below. [[Nona Black-Jaw]] shows that authority can be good while it goes unrecognised.

Earlier statements of the theme remain true facets of it. Rebellion against authority, grey morality, freedom from slavery and power that corrupts stand among them still. Found family versus inherited obligation, freedom versus systems that demand papers, tribute or debt, and the cost of choosing who gets to pass safely do as well.

Morality stays grey. The people at the bottom of society remain relatively innocent: ordinary people trying to survive and pay their bills. They leave the bad guys quickly once the heroes offer a better deal, as [[Geoffrey Draves]], [[Felix Aho]], [[Ruma Delacroix]] and [[Thunk]] did. People called bad or dangerous are often extremely good people who stand up for their kind, such as [[Nona Black-Jaw]]. A villain can be tragic, such as [[Hinewai]].

## Lines and Veils

A line never appears in any form: not shown, referenced, implied or carried as backstory or consequence. A veil may happen in the fiction, but the telling cuts away before the act. One sentence acknowledges it.

- **Line: self-harm.** No depiction, implication or consequence of self-inflicted harm.
- **Veil: hanging.** An execution or death by hanging may be named as an outcome. Cut before the act and never describe it.
- **Veil: sex.** It may be implied or acknowledged as having happened. It is never depicted, described or played out in scene.
